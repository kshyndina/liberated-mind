Defusion: put the mind on a leash

The yearning behind this pivot is coherence: the mind wants everything to make sense. Fused with our thoughts, we obey them as if they were reality. Defusion means noticing thoughts as thoughts, so you can take what is useful and leave the rest.

When to apply — the book's four signals
  1. Predictable thoughts. You have had this exact thought many times; it feels like part of you.
  2. Coming back from absence. You surface and realize you were lost in thought for a long stretch.
  3. Comparative, evaluative loops. Thinking turns into ranking, judging, self-referential scorekeeping that circles.
  4. "Over-busy" mind. A wrestling match of contradictions, self-commands and rules running at once.
Score yourself first with the Cognitive Fusion Questionnaire: under 20 means fairly flexible thinking; 20–30+ means fusion dominates and these methods will help most. Practice anyway even if you score low; the book compares it to physical exercise.
Two warnings from the book Never use these to mock yourself; defusion runs on self-compassion, not sarcasm. And do not believe the mind when it announces you have "solved" fusion; new thoughts to fuse with are generated daily, even after thirty years of practice. Progress, not perfection.

Starter set Do each at least once a day for two weeks

The first two build the general skill; the third and fourth target a specific sticky thought.

When: anytime, as a daily demonstration that the mind's power over behavior is an illusion.
  1. Stand up and walk slowly around the room.
  2. While walking, say out loud, clearly, five or six times: "I cannot walk around this room."
  3. Keep walking the whole time. Then sit down.

It feels silly and tiny, and that is the point: you just said one thing and did the opposite. The book cites a lab study where this one move raised tolerance of experimentally induced pain by roughly 40%. You can fold micro-versions into daily life ("I cannot type this sentence...").

When: daily, and whenever the inner voice starts dictating.
  1. Give your mind a name. Any name. (Hayes calls his "George".)
  2. Greet it by name, as if you were just introduced at a dinner party. Silently if people are around.
  3. From now on, treat its output the way you treat another person talking: you can listen politely and still not agree, and you don't need to win the argument.

If the mind has a name, it is not the same thing as you. That gap is the skill.

When: a specific problem-solving loop or worry starts chattering.
  1. Listen to the thought for a moment.
  2. Answer sincerely: "Thanks for that thought, George. Really, thanks. I see you are trying to help, and I've got this."
  3. The mind will likely fire back "this is stupid, it won't work". Thank it again for that one too.
  4. Optionally ask, with flat curiosity: "Anything else you'd like to add?"

Sincerity matters: if you say it with contempt, the mind just re-engages the problem-solving script.

When: a truly sticky, recurring thought has its hooks in you.
  1. Turn the thought into one sentence.
  2. Sing it to any melody (default suggestion: "Happy Birthday"). Out loud if alone, silently in company.
  3. Try different melodies, fast and slow.

Success is not the thought going away or becoming unbelievable. Success is seeing it as a thought, a bit more clearly.

Additional methods Return here after your first pass through all six pivots

When: one loaded negative word sits at the core of a recurring thought.
  1. Find the key negative word (e.g. "stupid").
  2. Say it reversed ("diputs") and notice the oddness.

The strange interruption reminds you that you are looking at a thought instead of from it.

When: a thought stays powerful and close.
  1. Place the thought in front of you and interview it: if it had a size, how big? A shape? A color? A speed? A strength? A surface texture? A consistency?
  2. If its grip has not loosened, switch target: pick your main reaction to the thought (a judgment, a prediction, "I hate this"), set the original thought aside, and ask the same questions about the reaction.
  3. Return to the first thought and re-check its size, shape, color, speed. It has often changed beyond recognition.
When: a hard thought needs distance fast.
  1. Say the thought out loud in another voice: a cartoon character, a politician you don't admire, a movie star.
  2. Try several voices.

Hard rule: the voices are for seeing the thought from outside, never for ridiculing yourself.

When: you need a physical metaphor for what fusion is doing to you.
  1. Imagine writing the thought on your palm.
  2. Press the palm against your face: you can see nothing else. That is fusion.
  3. Hold it at arm's length in front of you: other things become visible.
  4. Move it off to the side: you can focus on it when needed and still look ahead. That is the position you want with your thoughts.

Whenever a thought grabs you, ask: is the hand on my face or off to the side? Note that the thought never disappears; you simply see it as a thought and regain room to act.

When: a painful thought is part of your history and keeps returning.
  1. Write the thought on a small piece of paper.
  2. Hold it up and look at it like a fragile page from an old manuscript: these words are an echo of your history.
  3. Ask yourself: am I willing to honor that history by carrying this paper with me? If yes, put it in your pocket or bag.
  4. Now and then, pat the pocket: an acknowledgment that this is part of your journey and gets to come along.
When: a thought has deep roots in your history and you need self-compassion, not just distance.
  1. Pick a hard thought with old roots. Imagine yourself as young as possible while still holding this thought or one like it.
  2. Take time to picture that child: the hair, the clothes.
  3. Let the child say the thought. Actually voice it, quietly, in a child's voice (aloud if alone).
  4. Now imagine your job is to care for that child. Picture helping, hugging.
  5. Ask: how can I do the equivalent of that for myself now?
When: advanced. Only after the inner work is done and you are already good at distancing from a self-judgment. Never force it; pushing yourself out of the comfort zone before you're ready backfires.
  1. Identify a self-judgment you are ready to stop letting run your life (the veterans in the book wrote words like "dangerous" or "broken").
  2. Write it in big letters on a sticker or badge and wear it visibly, first among trusted people. It is a declaration: this judgment no longer rules me.
  3. Softer variants: share such a thought honestly in a talk or with your kids; listen closely when others share theirs and reciprocate.

Hayes describes decades-old shame dissolving within minutes of wearing the badge, and the group discovery that everyone carries the same secret words.

Bonus: the creativity dividend

Defusion widens the option space. The book's quick test of cognitive flexibility: pick a simple object, set a 2-minute timer, and list every possible use for it. Around 8–9 uses is typical; unusual uses score extra. Notice how fusing with one idea narrows you into a single category (that's "functional fixedness"). The inner voice makes a fine advisor and a terrible dictator.
