Values: caring, by choice

The yearning here is meaning. Values in ACT are not goals and not rules: they are chosen qualities of action, directions you travel rather than destinations you reach. They supply the motivational fuel for pivot 6.

When to apply When life lacks direction or motivation; before committing to any behavior change; whenever "should" and social approval have replaced caring. Start with the Valued Living Questionnaire: it locates the areas where importance is high (9–10) but your last week's actions scored 6 or less. Work there first.
Expect turbulence Values work stirs things up. The book predicts a few unexpectedly emotional, irritable or anxious days. If you fall into rumination and self-flagellation, run defusion, self, or presence exercises. If you hit resistance or procrastination, run acceptance. The reason it hurts is the reason it matters: we hurt where we care.

Starter set

When: the core exercise; do it before moving to chapter 14. Research cited in the book: writing about values changes behavior and health more than picking values from a list, partly by lowering defensiveness and stress reactivity.
  1. Take paper and 10 minutes. Write about one value that matters deeply, in any life area.
  2. Answer along the way: What matters to me here? What do I want to do so my actions reflect it? When in my life was this value especially alive? What have I seen when others honored it, or violated it? What could I do to express it more? When did I betray it and what did that cost me?
  3. Keep the focus on qualities of living you want to embody, internally chosen. No approval-seeking, no rule-following, no guilt-driven storytelling.
  4. If it turns into a "letter to Santa" (things you want to get from life or people), redirect to qualities of your own actions.
  5. Stuck? Rewrite what you already wrote until something new appears. You cannot do this wrong.
  6. Afterwards, mine the text: find concrete doable actions, and find the qualities you want in them (honestly, with love, creatively, with curiosity, compassionately, respectfully, openly, joyfully, adventurously, thoughtfully, fairly, supportively, peacefully, with humor, simply, reliably...).

The actions you extract are the direct input for pivot 6.

When: to anchor a value below the verbal level. Hayes calls the move "driving a nail into the wall".
  1. Pick a values area. Recall one specifically sweet moment in it: a time you felt fully alive, connected, in flow, supported or strong. Who was there? What were you doing, feeling, thinking? How present were you? Relive it as fully as you can.
  2. Hold (don't verbalize) the question: what does this moment say about the qualities of being and acting I want to bring into the world?
  3. Draw whatever picture comes. No words on it, no art critique in either direction: the point is escaping the verbal-evaluative mode.
  4. Sit back and reflect on the drawing: why does this area matter, what would living it require, can you touch what you want to stand for right now?
  5. If words now resonate, write a few under the picture. Put the drawing where you will see it: desk, wall, phone.
When: an area matters a lot but you are not living it, and the barriers are painful inner experiences.
  1. Take a high-importance, low-consistency area. List the painful thoughts, feelings, memories, urges or sensations that act as barriers to living it.
  2. Flip each barrier to reveal the value inside the pain. Probe question: what would I have to stop caring about for this to stop hurting? (Book's example: a client's felt worthlessness contained caring about dignity and protecting the oppressed; helplessness contained competence; loneliness contained connection.)
  3. For each barrier write: "If in [situation] I [feel / think / remember / sense ...], let it remind me that what matters to me is [value]."

Expectation-setting from the book: this does not remove the pain; it recruits the pain as a reminder of what you stand for.

Additional methods

When: a forward-looking variant of values writing.
  1. First think: imagine the next year is decisive for who you are. If during this year you became more truly yourself while still caring for the people who matter, what would that becoming look like? Where do you want to grow? What kind of person do you deeply want to be? If this year were a book chapter, what would its theme be?
  2. Then set 10 minutes and write about those plans and who you hope to become.
When: to separate genuine values from social approval and ego maintenance.
  1. Choose an action that expresses a deeply held value and do it in complete secrecy: an anonymous kindness for a friend, an unannounced donation, compassion for a stranger with no credit taken.
  2. The same day, do 10 minutes of values writing about what the experience was like and how you could do more values-based acts in ordinary life.
  3. Tell no one, ever. The point: you do what matters because it matters to you.
  4. If keeping the secret is hard (you catch yourself planning to reveal it later), that is worth examining: the need for approval may be crowding out your own meaning. Then do a tiny version daily until 100% secrecy is easy, and scale up slowly.
