Part III of the book, condensed

Applying the skills to real problems

The book's third part shows how the same six skills handle specific life challenges. This page condenses its guidance. Jump to what you need:

The book's own boundary For mental health struggles these skills are a complement to professional treatment, never a replacement. For eating disorders, professional care at the first signs. Use ACT to strengthen whatever evidence-based program you are in.

The general method for any new problem area

  1. Write what you want life to look like in the affected area.
  2. List the barriers: both internal (thoughts, emotions, memories) and external (situations, people). If the cause seems obvious (a critical boss), dig one level deeper: why does this hit so hard? Old voices often hide there.
  3. List what you've already tried. Then audit each attempted solution for hidden avoidance: skipping meetings, staying silent, overworking against fear, venting sessions that turn into rumination echo chambers. Avoidant solutions buy short relief and deepen the problem.
  4. Check which of the six yearnings the problem touches: feeling, belonging, competence, self-directed meaning, coherence, orientation. Feeling invisible points at belonging; feeling like a cog points at competence and self-direction; emptiness points at meaning.
  5. Run the toolkit through it: presence to get perspective, defusion for the negative chatter, values to see the mismatch, acceptance for what surfaces, self to soften the story, then one small committed step the next day. A week later, hold a home session and set SMART goals.
  6. Expect uneven progress. Some skills will need more work than others for a given problem; that is normal.
Two lifetime habits the book prescribes Do one new thing every day that requires openness and presence and touches your values. And do one kind thing for somebody every day; if you don't know where to start, do it anonymously ("I have a secret" style). Humans are deeply prosocial; flexibility spreads through giving it away.

Diet, exercise, cravings

The evidence highlights the book cites: defusion training beat direct craving-fighting by a wide margin in a chocolate-abstinence study; two ACT sessions raised gym attendance while standard advice lowered it; a one-day ACT workshop aimed only at weight-related shame reduced binge eating by about half. The pattern: shame and self-stigma do not motivate change, they block it. Hayes reports he could not lose weight until he dropped the self-shaming entirely.

  • Values first: beware values like "looking good" (self-objectification in disguise). His client's real value turned out to be getting her body back for dancing, hugging, skiing, playing; exercise took off once the finger-wagging stopped. On the treadmill, value-reminders outperform drill-sergeant self-commands, which measurably reduce effort.
  • Befriend the craving: step back, name it ("craving"), and interview it like an object: shape, color, size, solid or flickering, where it lives in the body, what emotion travels with it. Then imagine touching it kindly, even cradling it. You can carry a craving anywhere without obeying it; it is a part of you, not you.
  • Body knowledge: follow the science but test it on yourself; keep what works for your body, drop what doesn't (variation, selection, retention).
  • Micro-exercise everywhere: "a little now" beats "a lot later". Calf raises in queues, always the stairs, slow curls with the milk bottle while shopping, squats while coffee brews, push-ups every other day aiming at your age. When the mind scoffs that it's too small to matter, agree and continue.
  • Shame buddy: advanced social defusion; pair up with a friend fighting the same shame and wear the joke openly (their walking shirts: "Yes, my body jiggles when I walk. So sue me!"). A personal declaration of independence.

Stress

The harm comes mostly from our reaction to stress, not the stressor itself, and good stress exists (new relationships, competition, parenting). Fix what can be fixed (commute, hostile boss, workload). For the rest, the book offers Frank Bond's metaphor: you are a sink, stressors are taps; you can try to close taps, or you can pull the plug so stress drains through.

  • Sort your stress buttons: write each stressful situation on a slip, then sort into two piles: "can change the situation" and "can only change my relationship to it". For pile one, ask what stops you from acting and set SMART goals. For pile two, practice accepting and mining it for lessons; the job there is to react less, not try harder.
  • Defuse the roles: you are not your job title, your performance review, or your boss's characterization of you. Seeing that lets you hear criticism constructively. Perspective-taking also reveals that a barbed comment usually says more about the other person's stress than about you.
  • Stressor-linked habit: pick a frequent, neutral activity reliably tied to a stressor (the commute before the boss meeting) and attach a few minutes of defusion practice to it, e.g. singing the feared judgment. Within days the link weakens.
  • Passengers on the bus: you drive the bus called Your Life; memories, sensations and thoughts are passengers. Stopping the bus to throw them off means going nowhere; fighting them means letting go of the wheel. Write your rowdiest passengers on cards, carry them for a week, pat the pocket to welcome them aboard, and keep driving your route.

Sleep

The irony the book names: knowing how much sleep matters is itself a top predictor of insomnia, because trying to force sleep keeps you awake. First line of treatment remains CBT-i (sleep hygiene, regular schedule, bed only for sleep and sex, get up after ~20 sleepless minutes). Where ACT changes the recipe: skip the "restructure your catastrophic thoughts" part, which can backfire by making you engage the thoughts, and instead defuse from them and accept the tiredness. In the case study cited, adding ACT (leaves-on-a-stream, describing fatigue as an object, values work on avoided evening activities) restored energy and family life where CBT-i alone had relapsed.

Hayes' own falling-asleep exercise: a soft, blurry version of "open your focus". Let attention go wide and unfocused, note whatever surfaces gently and without interest, notice the empty spaces between events, and do nothing with any of it. Your body knows how to sleep; the exercise convinces the problem-solving mind it is temporarily unemployed.

Depression and anxiety

  • Depression: the engine is rumination, and the book's research nuance is precise: replaying a loss becomes toxic mainly when its function is avoiding the pain. Grieving with the pain allowed tends to subside on its own. Priorities: defusion against rumination loops, plus behavioral activation (scheduled, values-based replacement activities instead of avoidance patterns like oversleeping). ACT and behavioral activation combine well.
  • Anxiety: exposure works better on an ACT base: build defusion and acceptance skills first, then choose exposure targets by their value to you rather than climbing a fear ladder for its own sake. If a session overwhelms you or panic hits, that is part of the journey, not proof of failure. A practical tool: write the list of things your mind forbids ("too scary to: take dance lessons, travel alone, sing karaoke, tell a friend what he means to you...") and do one item weekly, running your mindfulness skills during it. Reverse-compass moves apply here too.
  • Psychosis (for completeness): even brief ACT training reduced rehospitalization in studies; the mechanism is the same distance-taking: symptoms influence behavior less when held as experiences rather than literal truths. Professional care is essential.

Addiction and substance abuse

The book's frame: substance use is very often avoidance, an attempt to mute hard thoughts and feelings, whether or not the person knows it. Avoidance only works fully while it stays invisible; the moment you can see the function, you gain a choice. ACT integrates with any evidence-based path: twelve-step programs (whose serenity prayer maps directly onto ACT's accept-what-you-can't-change, commit-where-you-can), methadone-assisted treatment (the early trial: 50% clean at follow-up with ACT added versus 12% without), residential treatment, counseling.

  • Start with values and return to them constantly: seeing concretely how use blocks the life you actually want is the strongest motivation to stay in recovery through the pain.
  • Acceptance for withdrawal and craving: cravings and abstinence symptoms trigger relapse; acceptance skills break the negative feedback loop. The practice of "urge surfing": ride the craving as a wave instead of obeying or fighting it. The craving-interview exercise from the health section applies directly.
  • Defusion and self for shame: addiction is among the most stigmatized problems, and internalized shame measurably worsens outcomes and delays help-seeking, often "until rock bottom". Distancing from the voice of shame and reconnecting with a self that is bigger than the addictive behavior makes room for self-compassion; an ACT shame-focused program added to twelve-step treatment kept improving results after treatment ended.
  • Triggers: you cannot purge every cue (thoughts of use can be triggered by a relative, a phrase, even the concept of abstinence). Rather than only avoiding triggers, ACT reduces their impact: room is made for the urges to roam the mind without being acted on, and their force fades.
  • The one-question compass (Kelly Wilson's, himself in long recovery): in this very moment, will you accept the sorrows and the joys, hold your stories lightly, author a life with meaning and purpose, and turn back toward that life kindly when you notice you've left it?
  • Small steps has an exception here: if gradual approaches have failed, the right commitment may be full sobriety at once.

Eating disorders

Serious ones need professional treatment immediately; that is the book's first sentence on the topic. What ACT adds to gold-standard CBT: eating disorders run on experiential avoidance (restriction, bingeing and purging regulate unbearable feelings, with anxiety at the core), thought suppression, rumination about body image, and rigid food rules. Values work re-engages people who are ambivalent about treatment by showing what the weight-control project has cost their lives; defusion loosens the food rules; self-work builds a base of wholeness that can coexist with thoughts like "my body is wrong"; acceptance targets the anxiety that restriction was medicating. The summary line from a clinician who has treated thousands: don't drown in the anxious details of the moment; define life's main values and follow them openly, flexibly, with curiosity.

Relationships, parenting, couples

The book's formula: intimacy = shared values + shared vulnerabilities. Flexibility skills applied outward: defuse from harsh judgments about others (and from their judgments about you), notice the stories you write about why people act as they do, take their perspective, accept the pain relationships cause and say it honestly instead of threatening exit, share values with each other, cooperate on SMART-sized changes.

  • Helping others become flexible — the formula "I'm RFT with it": Initiate, Model and Reinforce flexibility, From it, Toward it, With it. In practice: don't lecture ("you just need to accept your feelings" reads as judgment). Instead initiate by sharing your own pain honestly, ask open questions and just listen, model being at peace with your emotions (crying included), promise to stay present no matter how hard, and reinforce every small step the other person takes toward openness.
  • Parenting: parental inflexibility strongly predicts children's anxiety and how badly adversity hits them, and authoritarian control breeds child inflexibility in a feedback loop. The three-step reset for any moment you feel the surge of parental power: (1) come clean with yourself: what am I actually feeling — fear, exhaustion, an old echo? (2) take the child's perspective with compassion: treat their behavior like a story you co-author, not a math problem; (3) reconnect with your values as a parent: progress over perfection. On children's dark questions about meaning: normalize, acknowledge the pain, reframe it as the mind trying to problem-solve suffering, and anchor in chosen love rather than winning the argument. Persistent suicidal focus means professional help now. A daily habit Hayes uses: asking his son "What did you do today that was hard?"
  • Couples, two exercises: (1) each partner writes 10 minutes on their own values in one shared domain (parenting, money, home), no complaints allowed; then read aloud in turns with full attentive presence, the listener paraphrases back until it's right, then swap; share feelings afterwards without sliding into critique. (2) barrier cards: each writes one inner barrier they are ready to release (fear of showing irritation, old resentment), no hidden digs at the partner; take turns describing how the barrier feels, where it came from, what avoiding it has cost; the listener responds in order with an emotional reaction, appreciation for the courage, and one similar barrier of their own.
  • Abuse: if you are in an abusive relationship, safety and leaving may be the necessary action, and flexibility skills help do the herculean part: facing what is hard to face and changing what must change. Survivors' recovery programs based on ACT showed strong results; exposure-based and cognitive-processing therapies currently have the most evidence and combine well with these skills.

Work, study, creativity, sport

  • Motivation audit: write down the Dictator's performance-talk ("no promotion in three years, what's wrong with you?") and defuse from it card by card. Wanting to excel is healthy when the achievement serves chosen values, and corrosive when it serves fear-avoidance or image maintenance.
  • Procrastination is emotional avoidance, and psychologically it follows inflexibility. The three-step ACT counter: pause and notice the thoughts and feelings present; accept them and defuse; choose a values-based action, then set one small SMART goal to get moving. Grounding trick: touch the pull to procrastinate, find where it sits in your body, breathe into it for ~30 seconds.
  • Choking under pressure: the fix is presence plus defusion in the moment: feet-grounding works anywhere, mid-exam and mid-presentation included.
  • Cognitive flexibility games: speed-list uses for an object; write your opinions in opposites ("I hate artificial things; don't destroy them"); turn defeatist thoughts into metaphors, then flip the metaphor into a move ("I should just give up... like someone lying down on a couch. What if I got up and stretched?"). Perspective riddles ("if red were green...", "if I were you and you were me...") measurably build the skill, and the training has raised IQ scores in children.
  • Job shaping: refit your work toward your values in small steps: redirect attention to the satisfying parts, spend more time on them, and negotiate SMART-shaped changes of duties. For managers, the evidence formula: flexible workers plus a flexible work environment equals performance; model the skills, share real information including your own mistakes, and make rewards appreciation rather than crude barter.
  • The Matrix (Kevin Polk), a 30-second field tool: four quadrants: (1) what inside you do you most want to move toward at work; (2) what inside pulls you away; (3) what do you visibly do when moving away; (4) what do you visibly do when moving toward. Then notice who is seeing all four answers: the observing you, free to pick the direction. Memorize it and redraw it mentally in hard moments.
  • Sport: acceptance of discomfort outperforms both distraction and form-focus (the kettlebell study: ~25% more endurance). Rotate training days by skill: a defusion day, an acceptance day, a presence day, a perspective day, a values day, a commitment day (add the exercise you always dodge).

Spiritual wellbeing

  • Compassion meditation (record and play back): notice seeing, touching, hearing, then the one noticing; contact a painful emotion from the past day and wrap it in expanding, kindly awareness with each out-breath; extend the same wrapping to a stranger suffering somewhere; finally allow yourself to receive compassion as well as give it.
  • The eyes exercise: recall looking into the eyes of someone you know well; switch into their point of view and see yourself seeing; shuttle slowly between the two perspectives until the boundary softens into a "we". Hayes ties intense transcendent experience to holding both ends of I/you, here/there, now/then at once.
  • Forgiveness, self first: surface your judgments about a person without buying them or fighting them; trace the judgment pattern back through your history; find where you do the very thing you condemn; defuse from the shame that surfaces; then choose whether to give yourself the gift of a fresh start, and only then whether to extend it to them. Forgiveness never requires calling the wrong right; it is unhooking yourself from carrying it. The word itself: "fore-give", to give what came before.
  • Religion: ACT has been adopted across faith traditions (the book notes every major tradition carries its own ancient defusion technologies: chant, contemplation, dance, koans). The case study worth keeping: a woman with cancer who changed her prayer from "God, remove my fear" to "God, help me accept my fear and live my values", and returned to her life.

Illness, chronic pain, the end of life

  • Chronic pain: chronic pain lives in the nervous system like a memory, which is why even amputation usually fails to remove it. ACT does not promise to erase the pain; it reliably reduces the suffering and the pain's interference with life. Hayes' own practice for pain you can do nothing about: two or three minutes of breath-focused presence, contact the observing self, then gently move attention into the painful area, drop the rope of control and distraction, and find your way to "yes" for each sensation, defusing from intruding thoughts, cycling breath → observer → sensation.
  • Diabetes: in the trial, adding a few hours of ACT to standard diabetes education nearly doubled the share of patients keeping glucose controlled. The group exercise: stand before people you trust and "take a stand": say how you intend to act about your condition, what it reflects, why it matters, what happened when you forgot, then commit to specific actions.
  • Cancer: self-blame, forced positivity, and withdrawal from life are the traps. Map values against current actions, link every value to something doable, then use acceptance, defusion, self and presence on what surfaces. Choose the entry skill by the problem: rumination and anxiety → defusion and presence; shame and self-blame → self-work. In terminal illness studies, ACT patients spent their remaining time on meaningful acts (calls to children, letters, settling affairs) where the comparison group distracted themselves.
  • Tinnitus and unfixable conditions: Hayes lived it: three years of fighting a worsening ring, then one long walk applying acceptance, defusion and attention skills, and within two days the noise stopped mattering (it never went away; it became the hotel ventilation hum). In the trial, ACT nearly tripled the improvement rate of the standard retraining therapy. His summary of the end state: "There is nothing left for this noise to teach me."
  • Grief and dying: love and the pain of loss are one package. Flexibility skills help open the heart to sacred final moments instead of avoiding them; his account of his mother's deathbed versus the funeral he skipped decades earlier carries the whole lesson.

The book closes Part III with community-scale ACT: the Sierra Leone Ebola response, where trained local counselors combined flexibility skills with community cooperation principles (the "Prosocial" model) so villages could replace sacred but lethal burial rituals with a new form of honoring the dead, keeping both the community and its culture alive. Flexibility scales.
