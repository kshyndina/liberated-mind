Presence: attention you can aim

The yearning here is orientation: knowing where you are. Misdirected, attention gets dragged into the past and future. The goal is attention that is both flexible and voluntary: you can narrow it, widen it, and bring it back on purpose.

When to apply — the I'm BEAT check Use these exercises whenever attention is pulled unconstructively out of the present. To find what hooked you, run the book's acronym I'm BEAT: Interpretations, Memories, Bodily sensations, Emotions, Action urges, Thoughts (predictions, evaluations). Naming the hook is itself the way back: the moment you identify it, you are present again.
Practice logistics from the book Each exercise takes only minutes, so anchor them to daily routine: after waking, in the shower, at breakfast, midday, or evening. Consistency beats intensity; make a couple of them automatic enough to run from memory the moment you notice attention drifting.

Starter set

When: a few minutes daily; the base attention workout.
  1. Sit with a straight back, eyes slightly open, gaze lowered at about 45 degrees, soft focus on no particular point. Chair is fine, feet on the floor.
  2. Let the mind rest on your breathing.
  3. Every time you find the mind has drifted, release it from the thought stream and rest it on the breath again.

That's the whole exercise. Each catch-and-return is one rep: it strengthens both the noticing and the refocusing.

When: anywhere, anytime, even mid-conversation. The book often prefers it to breath-following, because breath focus can trigger panic in anxious people. Grounding through the feet interrupts automatic loops (anger, cravings, overeating).
  1. Set a 2-minute timer. Eyes closed, attention on your left foot: sensations, warmth or cold, pulse, the space it occupies. When attention wanders, gently return it.
  2. 2 more minutes: same with the right foot. Try to notice even more detail this round.
  3. 2 more minutes: hold both feet at once. Don't alternate; widen the beam to cover both. Redirect gently when pulled away.

The point is not relaxation; it is attention that is directed, flexible, and yours.

When: training attention on rich sensory input.
  1. Play music you like that has several instruments.
  2. Decide the focus plan in advance so the music doesn't steer you. Use a 1-minute timer per shift.
  3. Listen to the full combination first, then only one instrument (say, bass), then another (drums), then the whole ensemble again. Repeat the cycle once or twice with different instruments.
When: learning to widen attention, not just narrow it. Everyday use: meetings. A soft variant also helps falling asleep (see Applications → Sleep).
  1. Look around the room, focusing on specific objects one by one. Feel the sharpness of narrow focus.
  2. Then defocus from any single object and attend to the relations, the space between most or all objects at once. You can do the same with gaps between thoughts.
  3. Alternate for a few minutes and notice the two different attention strategies: soft and wide versus sharp and narrow.
  4. In your next meeting: switch between focusing on one speaker and on everyone in the room at once.

Additional methods

When: memories keep yanking you out of the present; training for staying stable with hard material.
  1. Deliberately call up a memory and frame it: "Right now I am remembering that..." plus one short sentence.
  2. Watch what arrives: emotions, body reactions, thoughts, urges, other memories.
  3. Label each arrival in the present tense: "Right now I am having the emotion of sadness." "I am having the thought that this should never have happened."
  4. If responses slip away, return to the memory and re-run it. Repeat for other memories that surfaced.

The thought or feeling may be about the past or future, but the labels train your mind to register that the reaction is happening now. This same "I am having the thought that..." frame is a powerful everyday defusion tool.

When: during any task (gardening, chores): trains double awareness of task and body without getting stuck on either.
  1. While working, keep attending to the task but move some attention into your body.
  2. Let a physical sensation step forward without taking over. Where is it? What are its edges? Hot or cold? Tense or calm? Pulsing or steady? Rough or smooth? Keep doing the task the whole time.
  3. Refocus on the task while staying aware of the sensation. How do the two relate: does the sensation shift with your engagement?
  4. Let any connections emerge by themselves. Don't turn it into problem-solving ("I must find the link!"); it is attention training, not diagnostics.
