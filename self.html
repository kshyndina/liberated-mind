Self: the art of perspective-taking

The yearning here is belonging. Misdirected, it makes us defend a conceptualized self, the ego-story of who we are, and that story isolates us. The pivot is a shift to the transcendent self: the "I" that notices, the perspective from which all experience is observed.

When to apply Whenever you catch yourself fused with a story about who you are, positive or negative ("I am smart", "I am broken", "I am not enough"); when self-judgments run the show; when you feel alone inside your own ego. Treat these exercises as gymnastics for the perspective muscle: regular reps, never one-offs.

Starter set

When: your first structured look at the self-story. Needs paper and 15 quiet minutes.
  1. Write three incomplete sentences: "I am ___". Complete the first two with single words naming positive psychological qualities you prize (not descriptive facts). Complete the third with a negative quality you fear you have.
  2. Interrogate the positives: is this true all the time? Everywhere? With everyone? Notice the mind's discomfort. Check whether each one secretly works as a comparison with other people (smart-ER, kind-ER).
  3. Change the period at the end of each sentence to a comma and add: "...or maybe not." Read each slowly. Watch what opens. If negativity floods in, use defusion ("I'm having the thought that...").
  4. Cross out everything after "I am" in each sentence. Sit with the question: who would I be without this quality? Would I still be me if all such sentences were open questions?
  5. Circle the two words repeated three times, "I am", and consider: maybe the deeper self you are looking for is closer to just those two words. Simply being.
  6. Final transformation: rewrite each sentence twice. First swap "I am" for "I feel / I think" ("I am loving" → "I feel love"). Then situate it: "When [situation] and I [my behavior], then I [feel/think] ___". Include situations where the quality is absent.

The situated form is the honest form: it shows when and how you act, instead of a global verdict on what you are. Keep using it whenever you catch a self-judgment.

When: a life-narrative ("this is why I am the way I am") keeps justifying stuckness.
  1. Write ~200 words about something you struggle with psychologically that has its own backstory. Describe the story and how it interferes, inside and out.
  2. Circle every reaction: thoughts, feelings, memories, sensations, urges, actual behavior. Do not circle explanations of why you reacted.
  3. Underline every external fact or situation.
  4. Now rewrite the whole story so that its theme, meaning, outcome or direction is completely different, while every circled and underlined element still appears in it. Not a happier story, not a truer story: just a coherent different one.
  5. Optionally rewrite it again later.

The realization the exercise aims at: with facts and reactions unchanged, the conclusions can change completely, because we are always authoring a narrative, one of many possible. Then ask: which storyline serves where I want to go? Who should choose it, the Inner Dictator or the observing me?

When: practicing being fully yourself with another person. Start with someone safe.
  1. In your next conversation with a trusted friend or colleague, bring full awareness. When the talk turns to you, watch for the subtle pull to lie: exaggerations, half-truths, confidence you don't have, pretending to know more than you do.
  2. When you notice the pull, meet it with compassion (you are caught in a human trap), then check: can I say it more honestly right now?
  3. If the conversation doesn't feel safe, note it for a later conversation instead. Gradually widen the space where you tell the truth.
  4. Also explore why honesty is hard there: the exercise opens a door to insecurity, inadequacy, fear of rejection. Use acceptance and defusion to stay yourself in the presence of those feelings.
  5. Advanced: deliberately pick a less safe person you have embellished with before, agree with yourself on one topic where you will give only true answers, and watch what comes up.
When: many times a day, cued by routine actions.
  1. Pick a cue: every time you touch your phone, keys or wallet (or set phone reminders).
  2. At each cue, ask: "And who is noticing this right now?" Touch the awareness for even a millisecond.
  3. If the question triggers a mental treatise on who you are, that's the storytelling mind; cut it off with a defusion move (hear the treatise in a cartoon voice).

Over time the question becomes natural and the connection to the observing self steadies.

Additional methods

When: 5–10 minutes, anywhere safe for reflection; repeat daily for several days.
  1. Let the mind settle on anything: an object, a sensation, a thought, a memory.
  2. Express it three ways, pausing between each: "I am aware of [X]." "I am not [X]." "My awareness contains [X]."
  3. With practice, shorten to: notice the thing, then "I am not this; my awareness holds this."
  4. Don't argue with the mind about it; just touch the sense that awareness is different from whatever it contains.
When: 2–3 minutes before a difficult meeting. The book suggests printing the prompts and keeping them at your desk.
  1. While you wait, ask: who is watching me wait? Touch that you are here, now, aware. Don't cling to it.
  2. Let a childhood memory of a similar situation surface on its own (no analysis). Watch it for a bit: who was there, what you did, felt, thought. Who noticed all of it then?
  3. You have been you your whole life; whatever happens in this meeting, you will notice that too. Promise yourself to stay you through its ups and downs.
  4. Now switch seats: picture the person coming to the meeting. Where are they right now? What do they see on the way? What might they feel? Think? Worry about? What do they deeply care about? What might worry them about meeting you?
  5. Come back to here and now: what worries you most? Then: is there a way for both of you to reach the deeper goals of this meeting?

This is the empathy expander; it builds connection with others at the level of awareness, not roles.

When: working with an already-lived painful experience; later usable in the moment. Record the steps as a voice memo with pauses and play it back with eyes closed.
  1. Close your eyes and contact the difficulty. Feel what you feel, think what you think, remember what you remember. Fix nothing.
  2. Notice that a part of you sees you suffering.
  3. With that noticing part, step out of your body and look at yourself. See how you look from outside while knowing it hurts inside.
  4. Hold (don't answer) the questions: "What do I think of this person I call me? Can this person be loved? Is this a whole person?"
  5. Move your awareness across the room; look at yourself from a distance. Note that nearby others are suffering too. Hold the same questions again.
  6. Imagine ten years have passed and you are wiser. From that wiser future, say two or three sentences to yourself about how to be right now.
  7. Mentally write yourself a short memo of that advice, return to your body, open your eyes.

People's memos tend to converge on flexible wisdom: just be you; it's okay and it will pass; you are loved; you can let this go. Your own awareness is your ally.
